What Predicts Brownlow Votes?

A deep dive into brownlow medal voting, using a statistical model to train and predict results, as well as a monte carlo simulation.

This project has two main functions that we’ll be looking into.

Firstly, what can a statistical model learn about Brownlow voting, and how accurately can it predict real results?

Secondly, what do the Brownlow medal results look like when those modeled probabilities are used to simulate thousands of possible counts?

The model is trained on 10 completed seasons (2017–2026), using basic statistics tracked by AFL Tables alongside coaches votes, SuperCoach and AFL Fantasy scores.

Historical model accuracy is measured using leave-one-season-out validation: meaning each season is removed from the training data before predictions are made for that particular season.

The Monte Carlo analysis is a seperate analysis. It takes the model’s estimated player strengths and repeatedly simulates Brownlow votes to produce a distribution of possible outcomes for the completed 2026 season.

Part 1 — The Brownlow vote model

What is the model trying to predict?

Based on the statistics for every player in every game, the model estimates the probability of receiving:

  • 0 votes
  • 1 vote
  • 2 votes
  • 3 votes

Those probabilities are combined into an expected-vote score:

p(1 vote) × 1 + p(2 votes) × 2 + p(3 votes) × 3

This score is useful for ranking players in each game, but it is not itself a literal Brownlow vote total. The actual Brownlow awards exactly 3, 2 and 1 votes per game, whereas the expected-vote score is a one-number statistical estimate.

The model is then converted into 3–2–1 predictions within each game. Those predictions are then compared with the votes that were actually awarded.

Is the model well-calibrated?

Accuracy and calibration measure different things.

Accuracy asks whether the model correctly identifies who receives votes. Calibration asks whether the model’s probabilities themselves are sensible.

For example, if the model assigns a group of players a 70% probability of polling at least one vote, a well-calibrated model should see roughly 70% of those players actually poll a vote.

Points close to the dashed line indicate that the model’s probabilities are reasonably aligned with what actually happens. Places where deviations occur indicate areas where the model is over or under-confident.

The vote-equity measure compares the model’s full-data expected-vote score with the actual vote total.

A positive vote equity index means the player’s statistical profile received fewer actual votes than the model’s expected-vote score would suggest. A negative value means the player received more actual votes than the model expected.

Part 2 — Monte Carlo Brownlow simulation

What happens when the model is used to simulate the count?

The model and the simulation serve different purposes.

The model estimates how likely each player is to receive 0, 1, 2 or 3 votes in each game.

The Monte Carlo simulation takes those modelled player strengths and generates many possible Brownlow counts. In each simulated count, every game still awards exactly 3, 2 and 1 votes.

This means the simulation produces a distribution of possible Brownlow outcomes, rather than a single deterministic vote prediction.

The win probability is the percentage of simulated Brownlow counts won by each player.

This is a simulation result, not a statement about the model’s historical accuracy.

What vote totals does the simulation produce?

The simulation gives each player a distribution of possible Brownlow totals.

  • Simulated mean = average vote total across all simulations.
  • Median = the middle number of votes received in the simulated outcome.
  • P10 = a 10% chance the result is lower.
  • P90 = a 90% chance the result is lower.

The simulated mean should not be expected to equal the model’s raw expected-vote score or the deterministic 3–2–1 model prediction, as those are different calculations.

Can the simulation correctly predict past Brownlow winners?

The simulation can also be backtested historically.

For each completed season, the model is kept out of that season’s training data. The resulting out-of-sample player strengths are then passed into the Monte Carlo simulation.

The test asks whether the player with the highest simulated win probability matches the player who actually won the Brownlow medal that season.

Across 10 completed seasons tested, the simulation’s highest-probability winner matched the actual Brownlow winner in 5 seasons (50%).

This test is deliberately different from the per-game accuracy analysis above. The model section asks how accurately individual game votes can be predicted, and the simulation backtest asks whether those modeled strengths produce the correct overall Brownlow winner when converted into simulated counts.

Conclusion

As you can see in the provided tables and analysis, the model and simulation results are not always going to be 100% correct, but with the Brownlow medal being a largely subjective award that was always bound to be the case.

I still think the model and simulations performed quite well all things considered. If you look at the predicted vs actual votes for season 2026 as well as the vote equity index, most of the top vote-getters were within 1-2 votes, with some exceptions such as M.Gawn, I.Rankine and C.Oliver (who all polled higher than consensus).

We were able to conclude that coaches votes are the single best predictor for brownlow votes, with disposals and goals following in 2nd & 3rd. This should come as no surprise, as many of the top vote-getters for the AFLCA Player of the year are usually some of the highest Brownlow vote-getters. Adding to this, goal-kicking midfielders are widely-seen as the most dynamic/important players in the game, so it’s no surprise to see disposals and goals as strong indicators.

Whilst the model was only able to predict 5 of the last 10 Brownlow winners, it had a 100% success rate when the predicted winner had more than 40% probability to win the award. Some of the past winners such as 2025 Rowell, 2023 Neale and 2019 Fyfe are widely-viewed as unexpected winners, and the simulations reflected that with each of them having less than 7% probability to win the award.

Thank you for reading this analysis, I hope you found it insightful!

Limitations

Brownlow votes are a small-sample and are a largely subjective outcome. This analysis shows statistical relationships and predictive performance. it does not establish why umpires award votes.

The Monte Carlo simulation treats games as independent and keeps each player’s modeled strength fixed across the season. It therefore does not explicitly model injuries, suspensions, changing roles or changes in form after the data was collected.

The simulation is also not a replacement for the model’s historical accuracy testing. Its outputs are distributions generated from the model’s estimated strengths, whereas the out-of-sample accuracy section evaluates predictions against actual Brownlow votes.
